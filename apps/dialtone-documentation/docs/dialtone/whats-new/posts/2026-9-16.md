Dialtone 10 is the largest change to Dialpad's design system since the packages were consolidated into one. It was developed under the name Dialtone Next, ships in September 2026, and already runs in production in the Dialpad app. This post covers what you will see, what you can now build with, what changed underneath and why that is your gain too, and how to migrate.

The changes form one system. The type scale depends on the token rebuild; the component refresh depends on DtText; the theming dimensions depend on the layered architecture; the utility fixes depend on cascade layers. The migration guides are ordered for that reason, and the post follows roughly the same order.

## How to read this

There is more here than anyone should memorize, and none of it needs to be. Everything is on the documentation site, and it is searchable from the site, the terminal, your editor, and your coding assistant. Three things are worth leaving with a real understanding of:

- The primitive triad: DtBox, DtStack, and DtText, and the shift from composing utility classes to declaring intent through props.
- The layout and spacing system, which is a breaking change and is built on two numbers you can do math with.
- The Mode Directive, which replaces every inverted-variant workaround with one attribute.

Make note of the rest and look it up when you need it: the four-dimensional theming system, the new and refreshed components, the move to OKLCH, logical naming, the redesigned documentation site and Component Playground, the tooling for coding assistants, and the migration guides.

## Theming has four dimensions

The previous theming system had light and dark, and switching between them meant swapping the entire stylesheet: one complete CSS file per theme, reloaded wholesale. That worked for two themes and would not have worked for fifty. Dialtone 10 replaces it with a layered system. The base theme loads once; every other choice is a small override applied on top, and switching is an attribute change with no reload.

Theming is now four independent dimensions, each set by a `data-dt-*` attribute on the root or by the runtime API:

| Dimension | What it controls | Values |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand | The wrapper colors: top bar and left navigation, built from five customizable colors | `dp`, `tmo`, and a set of experimental themes |
| Mode | Light or dark, including nested regions that differ from their parent | `light`, `dark` |
| Contrast | Stronger foreground and borders. Useful for accessibility and for a screen in direct sun | `default`, `high` |
| Material | The neutral palette: the black ramp that foreground, surface, and border tokens reference | `sandstone` (default), `steel`, `graphite`, `iron`, `amethyst`, `jade` |

Two of these deserve a word. Brand is deliberately narrow: it colors the wrapper and leaves the content area alone, so a brand change never fights with content. Material is new, and it works because black is the most utilitarian color in the system. Almost every neutral token derives from the black ramp, so re-pointing that ramp changes the feel of the whole product without touching a single component token. The default material, Sandstone, is warmer than pure gray; it reads as slightly softer in light mode and noticeably so in dark mode. Most brand themes lock to a hue-aligned material automatically; `dp` and `tmo` leave the choice open.

[Visual: the Dialpad Appearance panel switching brand theme, then mode, then high contrast, then material, one short muted video or four stills. Deck slides 05–09 are the source; sanitize the experimental theme names and anything internal-only.]

The runtime API follows the dimensions: `initDialtoneTheme`, `setMode`, `setBrand`, `setContrast`, `setMaterial`. The old `setTheme()` and `data-dt-theme` attribute are deprecated. The [theme-to-mode migration guide](/guides/migration/theme-to-mode/) rewrites the calls and flags places where a nested region should become a Mode Directive.

### The Mode Directive

Every product has regions that must be the opposite of their surroundings, or dark no matter what. The call bar. A hovercard over a light page. The calling experience, which is always dark by design. Until now each of those needed an inverted variant of a component, or inverted foreground and surface tokens, or a hand-maintained stylesheet of overrides. The inverted variants did not scale and the overrides fought the system.

`v-dt-mode` is one attribute on any element. It sets the mode for that element and everything inside it.

```vue
<div v-dt-mode:invert>  <!-- always the opposite of the parent -->
<div v-dt-mode:dark>    <!-- always dark -->
<div v-dt-mode:light>   <!-- always light -->
```

[Visual: three side-by-side panels, inverted, dark, light, each with a primary button, muted text, a critical action, and a link, under a page-level light/dark toggle. Deck slides 10–11. Inline demo candidate.]

Because it is scoped to the element, it nests. A dark call panel can hold a light popover that holds a dark tooltip. The component-level `inverted` props are deprecated in its favor, and [DtModeIsland](/components/mode-island.md) remains for the case where no wrapper element exists yet.

[Visual: the call bar with `v-dt-mode` on its container and nothing else, then a dark hovercard over the light app, then the always-dark call panel beside light content. Deck slides 12–15. Sanitize the product surfaces as needed.]

## Color

### The ramps, and their numbers

The base color ramps were updated, and the numbering that names them was rebuilt. The original scale ran 100 through 600. Every time a shade was needed between two stops, a number was invented for it: 350, 425, 450, 475, 550. Different hues ended up with different stops, and the number told you nothing about where a color sat in its ramp.

Every ramp now has the same twelve stops: 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 900, 950, 1000. A 300 in one hue has roughly the same intensity as a 300 in any other, and adjacent stops are visibly distinct. The hues most used in product were retuned; blues were cooled to separate them from purple; the brand-only hues were left largely as they were.

[Visual: the eleven base ramps before and after, then the same ramps with the old irregular stop numbers highlighted and the new normalized numbers. Deck slides 16–19 are the source, as a static pair or a two-step reveal.]

Two practical notes. First, the [color stops migration](/guides/migration/color-stops/) renames every old stop to its new position, and the [migration helper](/guides/migration/) then moves base-color references to semantic tokens where one exists. Second, and more important: reach for semantic tokens. Foreground, surface, and border tokens are the interface. The base ramps are what those tokens point at, and going to them directly should be rare enough to notice.

### HSL to OKLCH

Every color in Dialtone is now defined in OKLCH instead of HSL. HSL is convenient, and its lightness channel does not behave the way it looks like it should. Two HSL colors at the same lightness can appear very different in brightness depending on hue, so ramps built by stepping lightness drift in perceived contrast and saturation.

OKLCH's three channels, lightness, chroma, and hue, track perception closely. Across a ramp, lightness falls in even steps, chroma builds toward the middle stops where colors are most intense and tapers with a longer tail toward the dark end, and hue stays in a tight band while shifting slightly to keep each stop balanced. Ramps built this way hold their contrast and saturation from one hue to the next.

[Visual: one ramp with its L, C, and H curves plotted across the twelve stops. Deck slide 22.]

The second reason is plumbing. Under HSL, supporting opacity on a token meant breaking every color into separate hue, saturation, lightness, and alpha variables plus a composite, and reassembling them at the point of use:

```css
/* before */
--dt-color-foreground-tertiary-h: 214;
--dt-color-foreground-tertiary-s: 10%;
--dt-color-foreground-tertiary-l: 46%;
--dt-color-foreground-tertiary-hsl:
  var(--dt-color-foreground-tertiary-h)
  var(--dt-color-foreground-tertiary-s)
  var(--dt-color-foreground-tertiary-l);

color: hsla(var(--dt-color-foreground-tertiary-hsl) / var(--fco, 1));
```

Relative color syntax makes the breakouts unnecessary. One token, and opacity is applied at the point of use:

```css
/* Dialtone 10 */
--dt-color-foreground-tertiary: oklch(…);

color: oklch(from var(--dt-color-foreground-tertiary) l c h / var(--fco, 1));
```

About 3,000 custom properties left the system and the CSS bundle is about 33% smaller. Opacity utilities such as `d-fco*` keep working. The [HSL to OKLCH guide](/guides/migration/hsl-to-oklch/) removes any remaining channel-variable references from product code.

[Visual: the before and after variable code side by side with the two figures. Deck slide 23.]

## Typography

The default body size moves from 15px to 16px. Of everything in Dialtone 10, that single change is the most visible, because body text is everywhere.

The scale itself grew from five sizes to eleven. The old scale had 12, 15, 19, 27, and 38 with nothing between; the new one runs 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, 20, 24, 28, 32. The gaps are small enough that hierarchy no longer requires a jump of two sizes, and the largest headline is now 32px rather than 38.

[Visual: the old five-size specimen beside the new eleven-size specimen, same sample sentence. Deck slide 25.]

Not every size is meant to be reached for directly. DtText, covered below, exposes a curated set per kind: headline sizes 100 through 700, body and label sizes 100 through 400, code sizes 100 through 400. DtText is the intended way to set styled type in product; the typography utility classes (`d-headline--*`, `d-body--*`, `d-label--*`, `d-fw-*`, `d-fc-*`, `d-truncate`) are deprecated, and the [typography migration](/guides/migration/typography/) converts them to DtText props automatically.

## The primitive triad

This is the one section to leave with.

Everything in Dialtone is a primitive of some kind that you compose into a screen. Until now, the most basic composition, a container with some text in it, was done by recalling or looking up utility classes: which background class, which border class, which font size class, which color class. Manual assembly. It worked because the set was small, and it fails in exactly the way you would expect once a coding assistant is doing the assembling: it guesses class names, and its guesses are plausible enough to survive review.

Dialtone 10 changes the model. You choose a component that expresses what the thing is, and you describe it with props. The props are validated against the token scales, so an invalid value fails loudly instead of silently rendering nothing. They are discoverable, so an editor or an assistant can list them. Utilities remain for the rare case the props do not cover.

Three primitives carry most of this. DtStack has been in Dialtone for a while; DtBox and DtText complete the set.

**DtBox is the container.** Surface, border, radius, shadow, padding, and size are props.

```vue
<!-- before -->
<div class="d-bgc-primary d-bc-default d-baw-100 d-bar-200 d-p-200 d-bs-sm">…</div>

<!-- Dialtone 10 -->
<dt-box surface="primary" border-color="default" border-width="100" border-radius="200" padding="200" shadow="raised">…</dt-box>
```

**DtStack is the arrangement.** Direction, gap, alignment, and justification, and nothing else; it does not style the container.

```vue
<!-- before -->
<div class="d-d-flex d-ai-center d-jc-space-between d-g-200">…</div>

<!-- Dialtone 10 -->
<dt-stack direction="row" align="center" justify="space-between" gap="200">…</dt-stack>
```

**DtText is the type.** Kind, size, tone, strength, density, truncation, and balanced wrapping. `tone` replaces the foreground-color utilities: `tone="secondary"` rather than `d-fc-secondary`.

```vue
<!-- before -->
<p class="d-body--md d-fc-secondary d-fw-semibold d-truncate">…</p>

<!-- Dialtone 10 -->
<dt-text as="p" kind="body" size="300" tone="secondary" strength="semibold" truncate>…</dt-text>
```

Composed, they map onto the mental model designers already have. DtBox is the frame with its fill and padding. DtStack is auto layout. DtText is the text layer.

```vue
<dt-box padding="300" surface="primary" border-width="100" border-color="subtle" border-radius="400" shadow="raised">
  <dt-stack gap="200">
    <dt-text as="h3" kind="headline" size="300">Card title</dt-text>
    <dt-text kind="body" size="200">Card body content with supporting text.</dt-text>
  </dt-stack>
</dt-box>
```

## Layout and spacing, on two numbers

This is a breaking change, and the reasoning matters more than the rename.

The old `--dt-size-*` and `--dt-space-*` tokens were ordinal. `--dt-space-500` was 16px and `--dt-size-800` was 128px because those were the stops that had accumulated over years of product needs. The numbers were positions on a list, not values you could compute, and a coding assistant reasoning about them had nothing to reason with.

Dialtone 10 splits the two jobs and gives each a base:

| Token family | Question it answers | Base | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `--dt-spacing-*` | How much air? Padding, margin, gap, inset | 8px | `spacing-100` = 8, `spacing-200` = 16, `spacing-300` = 24 |
| `--dt-layout-*` | How big? Width, height, min and max dimensions | 64px | `layout-100` = 64, `layout-200` = 128, `layout-300` = 192 |

The stop number is a multiplier of the base. `spacing-200` is 16 because 8 × 2. `layout-200` is 128 because 64 × 2. `layout-50` is 32 and `layout-75` is 48 for the same reason. Between 0 and 200 both families provide quarter stops (25, 50, 75, 125, 150, 175); above 200 they provide half stops, so there is always a value halfway between two hundreds and you can predict where a stop you have not used will land.

```css
/* before */
.thing {
  padding: var(--dt-space-500);      /* 16px, because that is where it fell */
  inline-size: var(--dt-size-800);   /* 128px, likewise */
}

/* Dialtone 10 */
.thing {
  padding: var(--dt-spacing-200);    /* 16px, because 8 × 2 */
  inline-size: var(--dt-layout-200); /* 128px, because 64 × 2 */
}
```

The same numbers run through the utilities and the props, so the scale you learn once applies everywhere:

```vue
<!-- utilities: before → after -->
<div class="d-p16 d-w128">   →   <div class="d-p-200 d-w-200">

<!-- props -->
<dt-box padding="200" inline-size="200">
```

[Visual: a three-row table or graphic showing the same value, 16px padding and 128px width, expressed as a token, a utility class, and a DtBox prop, with the multiplication shown. Deck slides 35–37.]

Known gap: some product layouts use 20px and 24px blocks that fall between quarter stops. A separate block-size scale for those cases is under consideration.

The [layout and spacing tokens guide](/guides/migration/layout-and-spacing-tokens/) has the full old-to-new mapping, including the handful of old stops whose nearest new value differs by a few pixels, and the migration helper rewrites the token references.

## Logical naming

Dialtone no longer says left or right. It says start and end, because writing direction is not something a design system should assume.

The vocabulary is CSS's own: `inline` is the reading axis (left and right in English), `block` is the other one (top and bottom). It applies at every level:

| | Before | Dialtone 10 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Props | `padding-left`, `align="right"` | `padding-inline-start`, `align="end"` |
| Utilities | `d-pl8` | `d-pis-100` |
| Custom CSS | `padding-left: …` | `padding-inline-start: var(--dt-spacing-200)` |
| Slots | `#left`, `#right` | `#start`, `#end` |

The lint rules auto-fix utilities and custom CSS, so in practice you write it once and the tooling keeps it. And most of the time DtBox makes the question moot: its padding props are logical already. The [logical naming guide](/guides/migration/logical-naming/) covers props, slots, and events; the [border-radius guide](/guides/migration/border-radius/) covers the radius utilities, which moved to logical names and numeric stops at the same time.

[Visual: the three-column before/after for props, utilities, and custom CSS. Deck slide 34.]

## Component APIs

### One vocabulary

Component APIs had accumulated inconsistencies. Some components said `danger`, others `critical`. Some overlays opened with `show`, others with `open`. Boolean props were often phrased in the negative (`hideX`), so enabling a feature meant setting something to false. Size props took t-shirt letters that had no relationship to the token scale.

Dialtone 10 standardizes all of it:

| Before | Dialtone 10 |
| --- | --- |
| `size="md"` | `size="300"` (100 through 500) |
| `v-model:show`, `:show` | `v-model:open`, `:open` |
| `kind="danger"`, `type="success"` | `kind="critical"`, `type="positive"` |
| `hideThing` | `showThing` |
| `title="…"` on overlays | `header-text="…"` |
| `@input`, `@change` on form components | `@update:model-value` |
| `rootClass`, `wrapperClass`, `containerClass` | removed; use slot class props |
| `<a class="d-btn d-btn--primary" href="…">` | `<dt-button href="…">` |
| `<router-link class="d-link" :to="…">` | `<dt-link :to="…">` |

The last two matter more than they look. Styling an anchor with button classes was the workaround for a button that needed to navigate, and coding assistants reproduce that workaround constantly. DtButton with `href` renders an anchor; DtLink with `to` renders a router link. No class borrowing.

Numeric sizes deserve one more sentence. `100` through `500` is the same scale the tokens use, so a size prop and a token stop mean the same thing, and it extends without inventing a name between `sm` and `md`. The old letters still work as aliases until a future major.

These changes span several guides, each with tooling: [component props](/guides/migration/component-props/) for the rename pass, [component sizes](/guides/migration/component-sizes/) for numeric sizes, [success to positive](/guides/migration/success-to-positive/), and [link and button navigation](/guides/migration/link-and-button-navigation/).

### Slot class props

Customizing the inside of a component used to mean a wrapper class and `:deep()` selectors reaching into internal class names that were never a public API.

```vue
<!-- before -->
<dt-button class="foo">…</dt-button>
<style>
.foo :deep(.d-btn__label) { … }
.foo :deep(.d-btn__icon) { … }
</style>

<!-- Dialtone 10 -->
<dt-button label-class="…" start-icon-class="…" end-icon-class="…">
  Answer call
  <template #startIcon>…</template>
  <template #endIcon>…</template>
</dt-button>
```

Every slot that can reasonably need a class now has a corresponding `*-class` prop, and they are listed in each component's API table. That is the customization path; internal class names are not.

## Components, new and refreshed

Every core component was refreshed visually against the new type scale and tokens. Several were rebuilt or are new. The Component Playground on each page is the fastest way to see them; the notes here are what to know before you open it.

[Visual: a short muted video per component, or one still each, from the Component Playground: preset dropdown open, a prop changed, code updated. Deck slides 41–48 are the source clips for Avatar, Button, Tabs, DtBox, DtText, DtFilterPill, DtSegmentedControl, DtResizable.]

**DtAvatar** was rebuilt. Sizes are numeric. Color is derived from a `seed` (a user ID, hashed at render time, never stored) and an optional `family`, so the same user always gets the same color and the palette can be retuned centrally. Presence indicators use an SVG clip mask instead of the old border trick, which removes the halo that used to appear on hover. Presence states can show an icon for accessibility (`iconOnly`), and `deactivated` is a first-class state. The [avatar guide](/guides/migration/avatar-updates/) lists the prop changes; this one is manual.

**DtButton** gained composability: `startIcon` and `endIcon` slots (logical, not left and right), leading and trailing slots, slot class props, and `href` rendering.

**DtTabGroup** was refreshed, given a traveling selection indicator, and brought in line with the WAI-ARIA Tabs pattern.

**DtFilterPill** is new to core, promoted from a product recipe and rebuilt. It extends DtButton and supports a dropdown mode, a popover with custom content containers, deferred selection, read-only, and a clear action.

**DtSegmentedControl** is new. It switches views within a section and behaves like a radio group; DtTabGroup is for navigating between sections. It shares its traveling indicator with Tabs.

**DtResizable** is new: resizable panel layouts with handles and initial sizes, proven in Dialpad's calling prototype before landing in the system. It is the most technically involved component in the release.

**DtProse** is new. It applies typographic defaults to raw HTML you do not control: rendered markdown, help content, assistant output.

**DtTextList** is new: semantic `ul` and `ol` with marker, tone, and nesting props, replacing the `d-ls-*` utilities.

**DtProgressCircle** is new, and DtLoader was aligned to it visually and given a track.

Smaller changes across the set: DtScroller gains `before`, `after`, and `empty` slots and scroll-end events. DtHovercard exposes show and hide controls, and with DtPopover supports an external anchor element; DtPopover adds a `boundary` prop and keeps nested overlays inside a `<dialog>` ancestor's top layer. DtComboboxMultiSelect forwards input-level keyboard events and stops shrinking as chips are added. DtInput handles IME composition correctly. The tooltip directive supports Shadow DOM. DtChip's `interactive` prop now defaults to `false`; see the [chip guide](/guides/migration/chip-interactive/). The scrollbar directive's `never` modifier was renamed `always` to match what it does; see the [scrollbar guide](/guides/migration/scrollbar-always/).

**Rich Text Editor** received the most changes of any single component, driven by product use: a `@tiptap/static-renderer` Markdown converter replacing the custom one, phone-number linking, table styling and full-width tables, `preserveWhitespace`, `allowBackgroundColor`, and `allowLineHeight` props, Shadow DOM support, and fixes to toolbar focus return, arrow-key navigation near links, suggestion-list positioning, paste cleanup, and the bubble menu.

**Icons.** New: fax-inbound and fax-outbound, compass, circle and circle-dashed, filter-filled, agent-assist-color, and the density and line-spacing set. Updated: the hand icon, the star and sparkle icons aligned to the Dialpad brand star, and `dialbot` and `dialpad-analytics-ai` moved to the squircle shape. The icon package gains a `/vue` export.

**Localization.** Korean and Swedish are supported locales, and an unsupported locale now falls back to a supported one instead of throwing.

## Under the hood

Most of the work in Dialtone 10 does not show up in a screenshot. It shows up as CSS you no longer fight, APIs you can guess correctly, and behavior you inherit from the browser instead of maintaining.

**Cascade layers.** All Dialtone CSS lives in four layers: `dialtone.reset`, `dialtone.base`, `dialtone.components`, `dialtone.utilities`. Utilities beat components by layer order, not by `!important` or source order. Un-layered application CSS beats all of it. That last point is the one architectural change that can surprise an app during upgrade, so read the [cascade layers guide](/guides/migration/css-cascade-layers/) before you bump the version; there is a no-layers build if you need time.

[Visual: four stacked bands labeled reset, base, components, utilities, with "your application CSS" above them. Net new; a simple diagram.]

**Registered custom properties.** The variables behind the opacity utilities (`--bgo`, `--fco`, `--bco`) are declared with `@property { inherits: false }`, so setting an opacity utility on a parent no longer bleeds into every descendant.

**Softer specificity.** Component selectors use `:where()` so that your overrides need less force.

**Native dialog.** DtModal renders a `<dialog>` element. Focus trapping, Escape to dismiss, and top-layer stacking come from the browser, and roughly 134 lines of hand-rolled focus management were deleted. The DOM structure changed; the [native dialog guide](/guides/migration/modal-native-dialog/) explains what to check.

**Focus directives.** `v-dt-focusgroup` adds roving tabindex (arrow keys, wrapping, last-focused memory, disabled-item skipping) to any composite widget; six Dialtone components had their own version of this and consumers building toolbars and sidebars had none. `v-dt-focustrap` traps Tab within a container and restores focus on close, replacing five component-specific implementations. Both have introductions: [focusgroup](/dialtone/whats-new/posts/2026-4-15.md), [focustrap](/dialtone/whats-new/posts/2026-4-21.md).

**Recipes became UI Kits.** The product-specific composites (navigation, chat, call bar, forms, workflows) moved out of the core package into UI Kit packages owned alongside the products that use them: `@dialpad/navigationkit`, `@dialpad/chatkit`, `@dialpad/callbarkit`, `@dialpad/formkit`, `@dialpad/workflowkit`. The generic ones, FilterPill and ItemLayout, were promoted to core. The [recipes guide](/guides/migration/recipes-to-ui-kits/) maps old imports to new, and [UI Kits](/ui-kits/) documents what lives where.

**Vue 2 support ended.** Dialtone 10 is Vue 3 only. See the [Vue 2 removal guide](/guides/migration/vue2-removal/) if you have a lingering consumer.

## Accessibility, collected

It is threaded through the sections above, so here it is in one place.

Color-assistive themes for red-weak and green-weak vision (`prota-deuter`) and blue-weak vision (`trita`) and the high-contrast dimension are first-class theme values covering semantic and component tokens, not overlays applied afterward. `v-dt-focusgroup` and `v-dt-focustrap` replace eleven independent keyboard-handling implementations with two that behave consistently. DtModal is a native `<dialog>`. DtTabGroup follows the WAI-ARIA Tabs pattern. Focus-ring tokens gained an outset variant and an inset default so rings stay visible inside `overflow: hidden` containers. Avatar presence can carry an icon, not only a color. The documentation site's search and mobile navigation are keyboard-complete.

None of this relies on good intentions: accessibility tests, visual regression tests, and a bundle-size check run on every change to the system.

## The documentation site, and the Component Playground

The documentation site was rebuilt for Dialtone 10. Primary navigation moved into the sidebar. The site is responsive from mobile up, with a navigation drawer that traps focus, supports Escape, and returns focus to its trigger. Search runs in the sidebar: press `/` to focus it, arrow keys to move, Enter to navigate. Theme, mode, contrast, and material switching is centralized so you can preview any component under any appearance on any page. Every page has a Copy Markdown control (more on that below).

Component pages now lead with the Component Playground. Choose a variant preset, change any prop or slot, watch the render update, and copy the resulting Vue. It runs on 40+ components and replaced the static examples, which means the documentation and the code cannot drift: when a prop changes, the Playground changes with it. Prop dependencies are inferred, so options that do not apply are disabled rather than silently ignored, and sized props show the token value they resolve to. It is also the fastest way to learn the layout and spacing scale: set a padding prop to `75` and read the pixel value next to it.

## The docs are written for your coding assistant too

A person reads a component page once. A coding assistant reads it every time it writes a line against Dialtone. The documentation was reshaped around that second reader, and it changes how you should work.

Every documentation page has a raw Markdown twin, generated at build time with the interactive parts stripped and the API, token, and utility tables rendered as plain tables. Every page header has Copy Markdown, Copy Markdown link, and Open as Markdown. The site publishes `llms.txt` (an index of every page) and `llms-full.txt` (the whole site) for assistants that read those conventions. Callouts use GFM `> [!KIND]` syntax rather than custom HTML because the HTML version could not be parsed reliably by language models. Tooling documentation is generated from the tools themselves and fails the build when it drifts.

Two tools turn the docs from something you read into something your assistant queries.

The [Dialtone MCP server](/guides/mcp-server/) gives Claude Code, Cursor, and any MCP-capable assistant search over the Dialtone version installed in your project: components with full prop, slot, and event documentation, design tokens, CSS utilities, icons, and the guide prose for "how" and "why" questions. It filters deprecated items out of results and recommends the current alternative when it sees a discouraged pattern, so an assistant configured with it stops producing utility-class guesses and starts producing `<dt-box>` and `<dt-stack>`. Project-scoped configuration means one file in your repository and the whole team's assistants are Dialtone-aware.

The [Dialtone CLI](/guides/cli/) is the same engine from the terminal: `dialtone search`, `dialtone component <name>`, `dialtone token`, `dialtone utility`, `dialtone docs`, with `json` output for scripts. `dialtone prompt <name>` produces compact component context for any assistant that does not speak MCP. Both sit on one shared query core that also powers the site search, the [VS Code extension](/guides/vscode-extension/), and the language server, so every surface returns the same answer.

For contributors, the conventions in this post (logical naming, numeric sizes, token usage, Playground variant authoring) are encoded as rules that load into the assistant's context when working in the Dialtone codebase, so AI-assisted contributions follow the system by default.

The practical takeaway: if your team uses a coding assistant, install the MCP server before you migrate. The deprecations and the current API are in its index, and it will steer the assistant toward Dialtone 10 patterns while you work.

## Migrating

Every breaking change in Dialtone 10 shipped with its own path across: a guide, a codemod or migration script, and where a rename could be detected statically, an ESLint or Stylelint rule with auto-fix. The [migration guide](/guides/migration/) collects twenty of them in dependency order, and one command runs them.

```bash
# What does my codebase still need?
npx dialtone-migrate --health-check --cwd ./src

# Preview everything
npx dialtone-migrate --all --dry-run --cwd ./src

# Run the breaking-change migrations
npx dialtone-migrate --all --yes --cwd ./src

# Include the opt-in deprecation migrations
npx dialtone-migrate --all --include-opt-in --yes --cwd ./src
```

Of the twenty guides, eleven are breaking: color stops, HSL to OKLCH, layout and spacing tokens, border-radius names, avatar, recipes to UI Kits, component props, chip default, scrollbar rename, theme to mode, Vue 2. Seven are deprecations where the old names keep working: base to semantic colors, success to positive, flex to DtStack, link and button navigation, numeric sizes, logical naming, typography utilities to DtText. Two need no code change but explain behavior you will notice: cascade layers and the native dialog.

What the tooling will not do: decide whether your application CSS should adopt layers, find every DtAvatar usage (grep for it), review the nested regions the theme migration flags as Mode Directive candidates, or fix hover styling on buttons that now render as anchors. Each guide has a manual-review section for its own leftovers.

Dialpad Engineering is running the migration in the Dialpad app first, with the Dialtone team alongside, and then the Meetings product. The [Visual Changes for Designers](/guides/migration/visual-changes/) guide is written from that experience: what changed on purpose, what must look identical, and the problems that actually came up.

## What to do next

**If you design.** The type scale, warmer neutrals, and refreshed components mean every screen shifts, and that is intended. When a migrated surface reaches you, use [Visual Changes for Designers](/guides/migration/visual-changes/) and the running documentation site to tell a refresh from a regression, not the Figma component library, which has not been redrawn for Dialtone 10. Learn the triad and the spacing scale; they are the vocabulary you will use to specify and to prototype.

**If you build.** Run the health check. Work the guides in order; earlier ones are prerequisites for later ones. Install the MCP server first if you use a coding assistant. Expect the layout and spacing token change and the cascade layers change to need a human look; the rest is largely automated.

**If you manage a product.** Users see a warmer, more legible product with a refreshed component set. Nothing moves and no workflows change. Plan a design QA pass when your surface migrates, using the guide above, and budget for it as review rather than redesign.

## What stays the same

Rendered component sizes, border radii, and spacing values. The control drawings for checkbox, radio, and select. Every color behind a renamed severity word: `critical` is the same red `danger` was, `positive` the same green `success` was. All deprecated aliases keep working: t-shirt sizes, physical slot and prop names, `success*` tokens and utilities, legacy radius classes, `setTheme()`. They render and behave identically until a future major removes them, and the lint rules will tell you where they are long before that happens.
