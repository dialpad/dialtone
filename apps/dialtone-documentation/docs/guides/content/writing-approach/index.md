Words are integral to design. Consider them carefully because they can make or break the user experience.

These guidelines help us write clearly and consistently so users can feel confident and empowered using Dialpad.

Beyond these guidelines, we tend to follow [AP Style](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/ap_style.html), and we use [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/) for spelling in American English.

## Voice and tone

### Voice

Dialpad's voice is human, clear, useful, and optimistic. That describes who we are, no matter what we're writing, whether it's an email or an error message in the product.

#### We're human

We welcome everyone everywhere to use Dialpad, and we show this with our words. We're warm and approachable, but we're not overly familiar. We write the way people talk and have empathy for our users.

#### We're clear

We avoid industry jargon unless it's necessary, and when it is, we explain those terms. We're clear and direct with users, but never cold or uncaring. We're honest, even when we make a mistake.

#### We're useful

We're helpful, but we don't handhold. We make it easy for our customers to get the information they want and need. We teach people the "why" and "how" behind actions. We don't just tell them to do something.

#### We're optimistic

We take pride in our work and feel confident we can help our customers succeed. We're excited about the future, but we don't overpromise or make things sound better than they are. We're solution-oriented and use positive framing.

### Tone

Our tone changes based on the context. With tone, we consider our users and how they might be feeling in the moment. Be empathetic and meet users where they are when, for example, sharing bad news or celebrating success.

## Writing principles

Good writing is user-focused. It takes user needs into account and makes it as easy as possible to use Dialpad.

### Be conversational

Choose plain language that's conversational and easy for everyone to understand. Vary the length of your sentences. Use common contractions like "you're" and "can't." Avoid big words and jargon unless they're the industry standard.

Ask yourself:

- When you read it aloud, does it sound like something you'd say?
- Can you replace any words with simpler ones?

### Be clear and concise

Aim to be as specific as possible in the fewest words. Cut any words that don't serve a purpose, but be careful not to sacrifice clarity to the user in the process.

Ask yourself:

- Is every single word necessary to include?
- Is it as specific as it can be?

### Be consistent, but not at the cost of clarity

Prioritize recognition over recall. Use the same words for the same things across the product so users don't have to stop and think about what something means or how it works.

Ask yourself:

- Does the object or action already exist in the product?
- Are you introducing a new term?

### Guide the user

Be direct and use the active voice as a rule of thumb. Provide enough context to help guide users through the experience. Avoid permissive language like "you can."

Ask yourself:

- Is it clear who is doing the action?
- Are you using the subject-verb-object sentence structure?
- Does the user have all the info they need at that moment?
